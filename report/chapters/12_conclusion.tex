\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Conclusion}
This project set out to measure how much network faults lower the task success of remote inference on a low-cost
robot arm, how much of it the scheduling of Distributed Real-Time Chunking recovers against a client that simply waits,
which of its parts are responsible, and how the motion can be kept smooth.

We built a platform on LeRobot~0.7 that records, trains, serves and evaluates policies across a laptop, a
Raspberry~Pi~5 and two shared GPU servers; recorded three datasets for two tasks; trained seven policies with their
authors' recipes; ported \drtc's scheduling into LeRobot's runtime with four fixes; implemented six smoothing methods and
seven network fault types; and evaluated everything with frozen plans and raw records of every trial. With the Pi as
the robot computer, the seven policies reached 0 to 90\% task success without faults, GR00T~N1.7 and \smolvla{} the
strongest.

The answers are clear for the setting tested. A short fault mix lowered the success of LeRobot's patient client from
68 to 13\% on cube in box, because the client stops replacing lost replies. \drtc's scheduling brought success back to
65\%, as high as without faults, and on the bench it recovered from every fault of every type for seven policies on two
tasks. Its cooldown carries almost all of this; the latency estimator and the trigger matter little on a steady link,
and the same cooldown gives up replies that are only slow, so under a rising latency or over a slow WiFi link it does
worse than waiting. A stack of adaptive playback, temporal ensembling, a Butterworth filter and a resume ramp made the
motion about three times smoother on the robot without lowering success; RTC, by contrast, cost more in latency than it
gained in smoothness on this setup. Finally, a policy whose chunk is shorter than its round trip stalls whatever the
scheduler does: for those policies the playback and the link, not the client, decide the motion.

\section{Future work}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A cooldown for slow links.} \drtc's cooldown follows the replies seen so far, so it gives up replies that
      are only slower than recent ones. A cooldown that tracks a slowing link, or waits at least as long as the slowest
      recent reply plus a margin, would remove this failure.
\item \textbf{Compressed observations.} The robot computer sends uncompressed images (about 2.9~MB per request).
      Compressing them would shorten the round trip on WiFi and move the short-chunk policies towards the region where
      the queue can stay full.
\item \textbf{E4 with the RTC stack and more trials.} The robot experiment uses the stack without RTC; running it with
      RTC for the policies that support it, and growing every plan to twenty trials, would narrow the intervals.
\item \textbf{More tasks and policies.} Two tasks and seven policies are a start; harder tasks, longer horizons and a
      working MolmoAct~2 fine-tune would test whether the results hold more widely.
\end{itemize}
